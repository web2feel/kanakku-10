Why trigonometry?

Surveyors, sailors and astronomers often need a distance they cannot measure with a tape: the height of a hill, the width of a river, the distance to a ship. Trigonometry solves this with triangles. Measure one length and one angle, and the rest can be calculated.

a little history

Around 200 BCE, Hipparchus of Rhodes built a table of chord lengths, which is why he is called the father of trigonometry. In the 5th century CE, Indian mathematicians such as Aryabhata worked with half-chords, called jya. Arabic scholars wrote it as jiba, Latin translators turned that into sinus, and we still call it sine today.

This lesson recalls what you learnt in Class 9: the six ratios, how they relate, and their values at special angles. Everything else in this chapter is built on it.

The size of the triangle does not matter

All right triangles that contain the same angle θ are similar: one is just a scaled copy of the other. Scaling multiplies every side by the same number, so every ratio of two sides stays the same.

So sin 30° is ½ whether the triangle is drawn on a notebook or measured across a river. That is what makes the ratios useful.

tip

Try it in the Explore tab: tick “Keep the angle fixed”, drag the triangle bigger and smaller, and watch the ratio column stay the same.

check yourself

A small right triangle has opposite side 3 cm and hypotenuse 5 cm for an angle θ. A bigger triangle has the same angle θ and a hypotenuse of 20 cm. How long is its opposite side?

cm

Correct, 12 cm.

Not quite. Try the hint.

sin θ is the same in both triangles: 3/5 = x/20.

Both triangles have the same sin θ = 3/5, so x/20 = 3/5 ⇒ x = 20 × 3/5 = 12. The opposite side is 12 cm.

 You can type a decimal, a fraction, or an exact form like √3 or 10√3.

Complementary angles

In triangle OMP the two acute angles add up to 90°, so the angle at P is 90° − θ. The side opposite θ is the side adjacent to 90° − θ, and the other way round. Swapping the roles swaps sin with cos, tan with cot, and sec with cosec.

tip

Whenever two angles in a question add up to 90° (such as 35° and 55°), think “complementary”. The Explore tab has the textbook’s semicircle proof as an interactive diagram.

check yourself

Find the value of sin 50°cos 40°

Correct, it equals 1.

Not quite. Try the hint.

50° + 40° = 90°. Write sin 50° as cos(90° − 50°).

sin 50° = sin(90° − 40°) = cos 40°, so sin 50° ÷ cos 40° = cos 40° ÷ cos 40° = 1.

 You can type a decimal, a fraction, or an exact form like √3 or 10√3.

Some values are undefined because they would mean dividing by zero: tan 90° = 1/0, sec 90° = 1/0, cosec 0° = 1/0 and cot 0° = 1/0.

note

We write (sin θ)² as sin²θ, and the same for the other ratios. So sin²30° = (½)² = ¼.

check yourself

What is tan 60°? You can type the exact value or a decimal.

Correct, tan 60° = √3 ≈ 1.732.

Not quite. Try the hint.

tan 60° = sin 60° ÷ cos 60°.

tan 60° = (√3/2) ÷ (1/2) = √3 ≈ 1.732
